    Why the category forecast was not stretched to today

    We tested whether any of these public indicators could stand in for the missing April 2023 to 2026 category data, using only the years where we have both. The test is deliberately strict: whole calendar years, robustness checks, a correction for trying many indicators, and one real out-of-sample check on January to March 2023, which is in the data but was not used to fit.

    Sources: SEC EDGAR (Amazon filings), FRED republishing U.S. Census Bureau and BLS data, and Amazon's Q2 2026 earnings release. Segment detail and guidance were transcribed from that release by hand.

    Can we trust this forecast?

    How the model works

    • Model choice. For every segment we try four exponential-smoothing models (level, damped trend, seasonality, trend + seasonality) and keep the one that forecasts best when tested on data it has not seen (three rolling backtests, 12 months each).
    • Proportional scale. Volumes are modelled on a log scale, so a 10% miss means the same for a small brand and for the whole category.
    • Probabilities, not just a line. We simulate 1,000 possible futures from the model’s own past errors. Ranges and “chance of hitting target” come from those futures.
    • Calibration. Ranges are stretched or tightened until they would have contained the real outcome about 8 times in 10 in the backtests.
    • Scenarios. The levers rescale the forecast: sales ×(1+demand)×(1+price)^(1+elasticity). Elasticity is an assumption you set, not something measured in this data.
    • Limits. The data is Amazon review activity, a proxy for sales. Months after Mar 2023 are excluded (incomplete collection). New products with under 36 months of history are not forecast.

      How to read this

      • Age, not date. Products are compared at the same number of months since launch. A cohort that has not reached an age is left out of that age's figure (never counted as zero); the 2022 and 2023 launch rows are partial.
      • Launch month is Amazon's listed "date first available" (92% of products), otherwise the first review. About 6% of reviews pre-date the listed launch (variants merged under one listing) and are counted as launch month.
      • Same basis as the forecasts. Everything runs on reviews through March 2023. Newer periods are not mixed in, because that would compare different things.
      • Survivorship. Products Amazon has since removed may be missing, which can make older cohorts look healthier than they were.
      • Reviews are engagement, not sales, and follow sales with a lag; a product type with more reviewers looks more popular than it is.

      Why filling the gaps matters

      Year-over-year growth of the sales proxy. Amazon stops listing prices for older products, so using listed prices alone overstated the boom years and understated the recent decline.

      Share of the sales proxy resting on estimated prices

      Higher in older years, because older products lose their listed price.
